    Exercises Questions

    1.
    Consider the second-order differential equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'' - 3y' + 2y = 0. \end{equation*}
    1. Introduce new variables to rewrite this equation as an equivalent first-order system in two variables.
      🔗
    2. Express the resulting system using vector notation \(\vec{x}' = A\vec{x}\) for some matrix \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Let \(x_1 = y\) and \(x_2 = y'\text{.}\) Then compute \(x_1'\) and \(x_2'\) using the original equation. Remember that \(x_1' = y'\) and that \(y''\) can be replaced with an expression involving \(x_1\) and \(x_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    One possible system is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} x_1' \amp= x_2, \\ x_2' \amp= -2x_1 + 3x_2. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    In vector form,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ -2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) We introduce the standard substitution for converting a second-order ODE into a first-order system:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1 = y, \qquad x_2 = y'. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1' = y' = x_2. \end{equation*}
    To compute \(x_2'\text{,}\) use the original equation:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'' - 3y' + 2y = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y'' = 3y' - 2y. \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(x_1 = y\) and \(x_2 = y'\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_2' = 3x_2 - 2x_1. \end{equation*}
    Therefore the first-order system is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} x_1' \amp= x_2, \\ x_2' \amp= -2x_1 + 3x_2. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    (b) In vector–matrix notation, with
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    the system becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \\ -2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}. \end{equation*}
    This is the standard first-order vector representation of the original second-order equation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    2.
    Two tanks are connected in such a way that fluid flows between them. Tank A contains 200 liters of brine, and Tank B contains 150 liters. At time \(t=0\text{,}\) Tank A contains 30 kg of salt and Tank B contains 5 kg.
    🔗
    A pump moves well‑stirred brine from Tank A into Tank B at a rate of \(5 \text{ L/min}\text{.}\) Another pump moves well‑stirred brine from Tank B back into Tank A at a rate of \(3 \text{ L/min}\text{.}\) There are no other inflows or outflows, so volumes remain constant.
    🔗
    Let \(x(t)\) be the amount of salt (in kg) in Tank A at time \(t\text{,}\) and let \(y(t)\) be the amount of salt in Tank B at time \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Task: Derive a system of differential equations for \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use the standard “rate in minus rate out” principle. The concentration in each tank is “amount of salt divided by volume.” For example, the concentration in Tank A is \(x(t)/200\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Multiply each concentration by its respective flow rate to obtain salt transport rates.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The system is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} x' \amp= \frac{3}{150}y - \frac{5}{200}x, \\ y' \amp= \frac{5}{200}x - \frac{3}{150}y. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The concentration in Tank A is \(x/200\) (kg/L), and Tank B has concentration \(y/150\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Tank A: Salt flows in from Tank B at rate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (\text{flow rate from B to A}) \cdot (\text{concentration in B}) = 3 \cdot \frac{y}{150}. \end{equation*}
    Salt flows out to Tank B at rate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5 \cdot \frac{x}{200}. \end{equation*}
    So
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x' = 3\frac{y}{150} - 5\frac{x}{200}. \end{equation*}
    Tank B: Salt flows in from Tank A at
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5 \cdot \frac{x}{200}, \end{equation*}
    and flows out to Tank A at
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3 \cdot \frac{y}{150}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y' = 5\frac{x}{200} - 3\frac{y}{150}. \end{equation*}
    This yields the system:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} x' \amp= \frac{3}{150}y - \frac{5}{200}x, \\ y' \amp= \frac{5}{200}x - \frac{3}{150}y, \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    which correctly models the exchange of salt between the two tanks.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    3.
    Consider the system of differential equations
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} x' \amp= 4x - y, \\ y' \amp= 2x + 3y. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    1. Write this system in matrix–vector form \(\vec{x}' = A\vec{x}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Compute \(A\vec{v}\) for the vector \(\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    To write the system in matrix form, place the coefficients of \(x\) and \(y\) into a matrix so that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    For part (b), recall the matrix–vector multiplication rule:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4v_1 - v_2 \\ 2v_1 + 3v_2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The system
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x' = 4x - y, \qquad y' = 2x + 3y \end{equation*}
    can be written in vector form as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}, \end{equation*}
    where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    (b) To compute \(A\vec{v}\) with \(\vec{v}=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2\end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) perform the matrix–vector product:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Compute each entry:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 4(1) - (-2) \amp= 4 + 2 = 6, \\ 2(1) + 3(-2) \amp= 2 - 6 = -4. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    4.
    Consider the linear system of differential equations
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}' = A\vec{x}, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 5 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    with initial condition
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    1. Write the system in scalar form.
      🔗
    2. Solve the system for \(\vec{x}(t)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Because \(A\) is diagonal, the system decouples:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1' = -2x_1, \qquad x_2' = 5x_2. \end{equation*}
    Solve each equation separately. Recall that the general solution of \(x' = kx\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = C e^{kt}. \end{equation*}
    Then use the initial condition to determine the constants.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 4e^{-2t} \\ -3e^{5t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Since \(A\) is diagonal, the system becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} x_1' \amp= -2x_1, \\ x_2' \amp= 5x_2. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    (b) Solve each equation separately.
    🔗
    For \(x_1' = -2x_1\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1(t) = C_1 e^{-2t}. \end{equation*}
    For \(x_2' = 5x_2\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_2(t) = C_2 e^{5t}. \end{equation*}
    Apply the initial condition \(\vec{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ -3\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_1(0) = C_1 = 4, \qquad x_2(0) = C_2 = -3. \end{equation*}
    Thus the solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 4e^{-2t} \\ -3e^{5t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The system is completely decoupled, so each component evolves independently according to its exponential growth or decay rate.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    5.
    Consider the system of differential equations
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \amp -2 \\ 4 \amp -1 \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}, \qquad \vec{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    1. Find the eigenvalues of the matrix.
      🔗
    2. Find an eigenvector for each eigenvalue.
      🔗
    3. Write the general solution of the system.
      🔗
    4. Use the initial condition to find the specific solution \(\vec{x}(t)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Begin by computing the characteristic polynomial:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A - \lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 5-\lambda \amp -2 \\ 4 \amp -1-\lambda \end{vmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    After finding the eigenvalues, solve \((A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}\) to find eigenvectors. Each eigenpair gives a solution of the form \(e^{\lambda t}\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Eigenvalues: \(\lambda_1 = 3\text{,}\) \(\lambda_2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Corresponding eigenvectors:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Specific solution:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = 2e^{3t} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1e^{t} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Compute the characteristic polynomial:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A - \lambda I) = (5-\lambda)(-1-\lambda) - (-2)(4). \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = (5-\lambda)(-1-\lambda) + 8 = ( -5 -5\lambda + \lambda + \lambda^2 ) + 8 = \lambda^2 -4\lambda + 3. \end{equation*}
    Solve \(\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda = 1, \; 3. \end{equation*}
    (b) Find eigenvectors.
    🔗
    For \(\lambda_1 = 3\text{,}\) solve \((A - 3I)\vec{v} = 0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -2 \\ 4 \amp -4 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The rows are multiples; a solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    For \(\lambda_2 = 1\text{,}\) solve \((A - I)\vec{v} = 0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A - I = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \amp -2 \\ 4 \amp -2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    A solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    (c) The general solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = C_1 e^{3t} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + C_2 e^{t} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    (d) Apply \(\vec{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} C_1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + C_2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Solve the system:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} C_1 + C_2 \amp= 3, \\ C_1 + 2C_2 \amp= 1. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Subtract the first equation from the second:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} C_2 = -2, \qquad C_1 = 5. \end{equation*}
    Thus the specific solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = 5e^{3t} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + (-2)e^{t} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Simplifying gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 5e^{3t} - 2e^{t} \\ 5e^{3t} - 4e^{t} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    6.
    Consider the system
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -4 \\ 1 \amp 3 \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}, \qquad \vec{x}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    1. Find the eigenvalues of the matrix.
      🔗
    2. Find an eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue with positive imaginary part.
      🔗
    3. Write the real general solution of the system.
      🔗
    4. Use the initial condition to find the specific solution \(\vec{x}(t)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    The characteristic polynomial is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (3-\lambda)^2 + 4. \end{equation*}
    For \(\lambda = a + bi\) and eigenvector \(\vec{v} = \vec{p} + i\vec{q}\text{,}\) the real solution is built from
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e^{at}\big(\vec{p}\cos(bt) - \vec{q}\sin(bt)\big) \qquad\text{and}\qquad e^{at}\big(\vec{p}\sin(bt) + \vec{q}\cos(bt)\big). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    Eigenvalues: \(\lambda = 3 \pm 2i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    An eigenvector for \(3 + 2i\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{p} + i\vec{q}, \qquad \vec{p} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{q} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The specific real solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = e^{3t} \begin{bmatrix} \cos(2t)-4\sin(2t) \\ 2\cos(2t)+\frac{1}{2}\sin(2t) \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Compute eigenvalues.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A - \lambda I) = (3-\lambda)^2 - (-4)(1) = (3-\lambda)^2 + 4. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} (3-\lambda)^2 + 4 = \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 13. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \lambda = \frac{6 \pm \sqrt{36 - 52}}{2} = 3 \pm 2i. \end{equation*}
    (b) Eigenvector for \(\lambda = 3 + 2i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A - (3+2i)I = \begin{bmatrix} -2i \amp -4 \\ 1 \amp -2i \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    First row:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -2i\,v_1 - 4v_2 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_1 = 2i\,v_2. \end{equation*}
    Choose \(v_2=1\text{.}\) Then \(v_1 = 2i\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So an eigenvector is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 2i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \vec{p} + i\vec{q}, \qquad \vec{p}=\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}, \quad \vec{q}=\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    (c) General real solution.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = e^{3t} \Big( C_1(\vec{p}\cos(2t) - \vec{q}\sin(2t)) + C_2(\vec{p}\sin(2t) + \vec{q}\cos(2t)) \Big). \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\vec{p}\) and \(\vec{q}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = e^{3t} \left[ C_1 \begin{bmatrix} -2\sin(2t) \\ \cos(2t) \end{bmatrix} + C_2 \begin{bmatrix} 2\cos(2t) \\ \sin(2t) \end{bmatrix} \right]. \end{equation*}
    (d) Apply \(\vec{x}(0)=\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} C_1\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} + C_2\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    From the second component: \(C_1 = 2.\)
    🔗
    From the first component:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2C_2 = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_2 = \tfrac12. \end{equation*}
    Thus the specific solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}(t) = e^{3t} \begin{bmatrix} \cos(2t)-4\sin(2t) \\ 2\cos(2t)+\frac{1}{2}\sin(2t) \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    7.
    Consider the autonomous system
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x}' = A\vec{x}, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp -3 \\ 1 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    1. Compute the eigenvalues of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Classify the equilibrium at \(\vec{x}=\vec{0}\) (node, spiral, saddle, center).
      🔗
    3. Determine whether the equilibrium is stable or unstable.
      🔗
    4. Describe qualitatively the behavior of trajectories.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute \(\det(A - \lambda I)\) directly:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A-\lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 1-\lambda \amp -3 \\ 1 \amp 1-\lambda \end{vmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Solve for the eigenvalues. Use the real part of the eigenvalues to determine stability.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The eigenvalues are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda = 1 \pm i\sqrt{3}. \end{equation*}
    Since the real part is positive, the equilibrium is an unstable spiral source.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Compute eigenvalues.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \det(A-\lambda I) = (1-\lambda)(1-\lambda) - (-3)(1) = (1-\lambda)^2 + 3. \end{equation*}
    Expand:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (1-\lambda)^2 + 3 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 1 + 3 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 4. \end{equation*}
    Solve:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 16}}{2} = 1 \pm i\sqrt{3}. \end{equation*}
    (b) Classification.
    🔗
    The eigenvalues are complex (non-real), so the origin is a spiral equilibrium.
    🔗
    (c) Stability.
    🔗
    The real part of both eigenvalues is \(1 > 0\text{.}\) Therefore solutions spiral away from the origin: the equilibrium is an unstable spiral.
    🔗
    (d) Qualitative behavior.
    🔗
    • Solutions spiral outward as \(t \to \infty\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • The imaginary part \(\sqrt{3}\) causes oscillations.
      🔗
    • The positive real part produces exponential growth in radius.
      🔗
    • Rotation is counterclockwise because the off-diagonal entries produce that turning direction.
      🔗
    The phase portrait in Figure 39 shows several outward‑spiraling trajectories along with the direction field.
