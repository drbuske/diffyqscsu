    Exercises Questions

    1.
    Using only the definition,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}(s) = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-st} f(t)\, dt, \end{equation*}
    compute the Laplace transform of the function \(f(t) = t e^{-2t}.\)
    🔗
    Do not use a Laplace transform table; evaluate the integral by hand.
    🔗
    Hint.
    You should compute
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-st} e^{-2t}\, dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-(s+2)t}\, dt. \end{equation*}
    Recall the standard integral (integration by parts):
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-a t}\, dt = \frac{1}{a^{2}}, \qquad \text{for } a > 0. \end{equation*}
    Here, \(a = s+2\text{.}\) Make sure to justify why \(s+2 > 0\) is required for convergence.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    For \(s > -2\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{t e^{-2t}\}(s) = \frac{1}{(s+2)^{2}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    By definition of the Laplace transform,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathcal{L}\{t e^{-2t}\}(s) \amp = \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-st} e^{-2t}\, dt \\ \amp = \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-(s+2)t}\, dt. \end{align*}
    For the integral to converge, we require \(s+2 > 0\text{.}\) Let \(a = s+2\text{.}\) Then the integral becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-a t}\, dt. \end{equation*}
    Integrate by parts:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int_{0}^{\infty} t e^{-a t}\, dt \amp = \left[ -\frac{t}{a} e^{-a t} \right]_{0}^{\infty} + \frac{1}{a} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-a t}\, dt \\[1ex] \amp = 0 + \frac{1}{a} \left[ -\frac{1}{a} e^{-a t} \right]_{0}^{\infty} \\[1ex] \amp = \frac{1}{a^{2}}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(a = s+2\text{,}\) we obtain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{t e^{-2t}\}(s) = \frac{1}{(s+2)^{2}}. \end{equation*}
    This is valid for \(s > -2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    2.
    Compute the inverse Laplace transform of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(s) = \frac{3}{s-2} \;+\; \frac{5}{s^{2}+4}. \end{equation*}
    Hint.
    Recall the standard Laplace transform identities.
    🔗
    • \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\} = \dfrac{1}{s-a}\) for \(s > a\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • \(\mathcal{L}\{\sin(bt)\} = \dfrac{b}{s^{2}+b^{2}}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    Match each term of \(F(s)\) with one of these standard forms.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(f(t) = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}(t) = 3e^{2t} + \tfrac{5}{2}\sin(2t).\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We compute the inverse Laplace transform term by term.
    🔗
    First,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3}{s-2} \quad\longrightarrow\quad 3e^{2t}, \end{equation*}
    since \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{2t}\} = \dfrac{1}{s-2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5}{s^{2}+4}. \end{equation*}
    Note that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{\sin(2t)\} = \frac{2}{s^{2}+4}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5}{s^{2}+4} = \frac{5}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{s^{2}+4} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \frac{5}{2}\sin(2t). \end{equation*}
    Combining both parts gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(t) = \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}(t) = 3e^{2t} + \frac{5}{2}\sin(2t). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    3.
    Use the Laplace transform to solve the initial value problem
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y' + 3y = e^{-2t}, \qquad y(0) = 5. \end{equation*}
    Hint.
    Recall the Laplace transform of a first derivative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{y'\}(s) = sY(s) - y(0). \end{equation*}
    Also use that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{e^{-2t}\}(s) = \frac{1}{s+2}. \end{equation*}
    After applying the Laplace transform, solve for \(Y(s)\) and rewrite it in a form suitable for taking an inverse transform.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The solution to the IVP is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = 4e^{-3t} + e^{-2t}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Take the Laplace transform of both sides of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y' + 3y = e^{-2t}, \end{equation*}
    using \(y(0)=5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First compute \(\mathcal{L}\{y'\}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{y'\}(s) = sY(s) - y(0) = sY(s) - 5. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} sY(s) - 5 + 3Y(s) = \frac{1}{s+2}. \end{equation*}
    Combine terms:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (s + 3)Y(s) - 5 = \frac{1}{s+2}. \end{equation*}
    Solve for \(Y(s)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (s+3)Y(s) = \frac{1}{s+2} + 5 = \frac{1 + 5(s+2)}{s+2} = \frac{5s + 11}{s+2}. \end{equation*}
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Y(s) = \frac{5s + 11}{(s+3)(s+2)}. \end{equation*}
    Perform partial fraction decomposition. Write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{5s + 11}{(s+3)(s+2)} = \frac{A}{s+3} + \frac{B}{s+2}. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5s + 11 = A(s+2) + B(s+3). \end{equation*}
    Expand:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 5s + 11 = (A+B)s + (2A + 3B). \end{equation*}
    Match coefficients:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A + B = 5, \qquad 2A + 3B = 11. \end{equation*}
    Solve the system:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = 4, \qquad B = 1. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Y(s) = \frac{4}{s+3} + \frac{1}{s+2}. \end{equation*}
    Take inverse Laplace transforms term by term:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{4}{s+3}\right\} = 4e^{-3t}, \qquad \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+2}\right\} = e^{-2t}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore the solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = 4e^{-3t} + e^{-2t}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    4.
    Using the convolution theorem, compute the inverse Laplace transform of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(s) = \frac{1}{s(s+2)}. \end{equation*}
    Do not use partial fractions. Instead, express \(F(s)\) as a product of simpler transforms and use the convolution formula.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Recall that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{1\}(s) = \frac{1}{s}, \qquad \mathcal{L}\{e^{-2t}\}(s) = \frac{1}{s+2}. \end{equation*}
    The convolution theorem states:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)G(s)\} = (f * g)(t) = \int_{0}^{t} f(\tau)\, g(t-\tau)\, d\tau. \end{equation*}
    Use \(f(t)=1\) and \(g(t)=e^{-2t}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left\{\dfrac{1}{s(s+2)}\right\}(t) = \frac{1 - e^{-2t}}{2}.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We begin by writing
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(s) = \frac{1}{s} \cdot \frac{1}{s+2}. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s}\right\} = 1, \qquad \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+2}\right\} = e^{-2t}. \end{equation*}
    By the convolution theorem,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}(t) = (1 * e^{-2t})(t) = \int_{0}^{t} 1 \cdot e^{-2(t-\tau)}\, d\tau. \end{equation*}
    Compute the integral:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{t} e^{-2(t-\tau)}\, d\tau = e^{-2t} \int_{0}^{t} e^{2\tau}\, d\tau. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = e^{-2t} \left[\frac{1}{2} e^{2\tau}\right]_{0}^{t} = e^{-2t} \left(\frac{1}{2}e^{2t} - \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1 - e^{-2t}}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left\{\frac{1}{s(s+2)}\right\}(t) = \frac{1 - e^{-2t}}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    5.
    Consider the initial value problem
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'' + 4y = \delta(t - \pi), \qquad y(0) = 0,\; y'(0) = 0. \end{equation*}
    Use the Laplace transform and the identity
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-a)\} = e^{-as}, \end{equation*}
    to find the solution \(y(t)\text{.}\) Your final answer should be written using a unit step function \(u(t-\pi)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Let \(Y(s) = \mathcal{L}\{y(t)\}\text{.}\) Use the Laplace transform formulas:
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \mathcal{L}\{y''\} = s^{2}Y(s) - s y(0) - y'(0)\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \mathcal{L}\{y\} = Y(s)\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-\pi)\} = e^{-\pi s}\)
      🔗
    After solving for \(Y(s)\text{,}\) recall that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left\{\frac{1}{s^{2}+4}\right\} = \frac{1}{2}\sin(2t) \end{equation*}
    and apply the time‑shift rule
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{e^{-as}F(s)\} = u(t-a)\, f(t-a). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    The solution is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, u(t-\pi)\, \sin\!\bigl(2(t-\pi)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Apply the Laplace transform to both sides of
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y'' + 4y = \delta(t-\pi), \qquad y(0)=0,\; y'(0)=0. \end{equation*}
    Compute \(\mathcal{L}\{y''\}\) using the initial conditions:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}\{y''\} = s^{2}Y(s) - s y(0) - y'(0) = s^{2}Y(s). \end{equation*}
    Thus the transformed equation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} s^{2}Y(s) + 4Y(s) = e^{-\pi s}. \end{equation*}
    Factor out \(Y(s)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (s^{2} + 4) Y(s) = e^{-\pi s}. \end{equation*}
    So
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Y(s) = \frac{e^{-\pi s}}{s^{2} + 4}. \end{equation*}
    Now apply the inverse Laplace transform. We use the time‑shift rule:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left\{\frac{1}{s^{2}+4}\right\} = \frac{1}{2}\sin(2t), \end{equation*}
    hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\left\{\frac{e^{-\pi s}}{s^{2}+4}\right\} = u(t-\pi)\,\frac{1}{2}\sin\!\bigl(2(t-\pi)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the solution to the IVP is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, u(t-\pi)\, \sin\!\bigl(2(t-\pi)\bigr). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    6.
    Vectors \(\vec{u}\) (blue) and \(\vec{v}\) (red) appear in Figure 36. Draw the vector \(\vec{u} - \tfrac12\,\vec{v}\) on the same grid.
    🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    First construct \(\tfrac12\,\vec{v}\) (same direction as \(\vec{v}\) but half the length). Then reverse it to obtain \(-\tfrac12\,\vec{v}\text{.}\) Place the tail of \(-\tfrac12\,\vec{v}\) at the tip of \(\vec{u}\text{;}\) the vector from the origin to the new tip is \(\vec{u} - \tfrac12\,\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.

    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We sketch \(\tfrac12\,\vec{v}\) (half the red vector in the same direction), reverse it to get \(-\tfrac12\,\vec{v}\text{,}\) and place its tail at the tip of \(\vec{u}\text{.}\) The vector from the origin to that final tip is \(\vec{w}=\vec{u} - \tfrac12\,\vec{v}\text{,}\) drawn here in black.

    7.
    Compute the magnitude of the vector \(\vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -4 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix}.\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Recall that the magnitude of a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|\vec{w}\| = \sqrt{w_1^2 + w_2^2 + w_3^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\|\vec{w}\| = 13.\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the formula
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|\vec{w}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2 + 12^2}, \end{equation*}
    compute:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3^2 = 9,\qquad (-4)^2 = 16,\qquad 12^2 = 144. \end{equation*}
    Sum the squares:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 9 + 16 + 144 = 169. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|\vec{w}\| = \sqrt{169} = 13. \end{equation*}

    8.
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \amp -1 \\ 3 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 5 \\ -2 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Compute each of the following:
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle A + B\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle 2A - B\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle A\vec{v}\)
      🔗
    4. Compute the inverse \(A^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    Hint.
    For matrix addition, subtraction, or scalar multiplication, work entry‑wise. For \(A\vec{v}\text{,}\) use
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11}v_1 + a_{12}v_2 \\ a_{21}v_1 + a_{22}v_2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    For the inverse of a \(2\times 2\) matrix
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a \amp b \\ c \amp d \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    use the formula
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d \amp -b \\ -c \amp a \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{provided } ad - bc \neq 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 4 \\ 1 \amp 4 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(2A - B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp -7 \\ 8 \amp 8 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) \(A\vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\text{,}\) and
    🔗

    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 4 \amp -2 \\ 3 \amp 6 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}

    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 2 \amp 4 \amp -2 \\ 3 \amp 6 \amp -3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 - 2R_1,\; R_3 - 3R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \amp 2 \amp -1 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
