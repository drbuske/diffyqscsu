    Handout Daily Prep 5.5 - Two-Dimensional Systems and their Vector Fields
    

    Section Overview

    We now use two-dimensional autonomous systems of the form \(\vec{x}' = P\vec{x}\) and show how their qualitative behavior can be understood through vector fields in the phase plane. Each point \((x_1, x_2)\) in the plane determines a direction vector \(P\vec{x}\text{,}\) producing a picture of how solutions move over time. Because the underlying system is linear and autonomous, equilibrium points occur where \(P\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{,}\) typically the origin, and the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(P\) determine the geometry of nearby trajectories. Solutions tangent to eigenvectors form principal solution curves, while the signs and types of eigenvalues (real, repeated, or complex) determine whether the origin behaves as a node, saddle, spiral, or center. We then illustrate how the eigenvalue method combines with vector-field intuition to classify the qualitative behavior of all solutions in the plane.

    Exercises Exercises

    1. Understanding the Vector Field.
    For a two-dimensional linear system \(\vec{x}' = P\vec{x}\text{,}\) what does the vector field in the phase plane represent?
    🔗
    • At each point \(\vec{x}\text{,}\) the vector field assigns the direction vector \(P\vec{x}\text{.}\)
    • Correct. The flow direction at each point in the plane is given by \(P\vec{x}\text{.}\)
    • It assigns the length \(\|\vec{x}\|\) at each point.
    • No. Vector fields assign direction vectors, not lengths.
    • It assigns the scalar \(\det(P)\) to every point.
    • No. The determinant plays a role in stability, not in defining the field.
    🔗
    2. Identifying the Equilibrium Point.
    For the homogeneous linear system \(\vec{x}' = P\vec{x}\text{,}\) what is the equilibrium point?
    🔗
    • \(\vec{x} = \vec{0}\)
    • Correct. In a homogeneous system, the equilibrium is the solution to \(P\vec{x} = \vec{0}\text{,}\) which is typically only the origin.
    • \(\vec{x} = \vec{v}_1\text{,}\) where \(\vec{v}_1\) is any eigenvector.
    • No. Eigenvectors describe trajectory directions, not equilibria.
    • Every point in the plane is an equilibrium.
    • No. That occurs only in the trivial zero matrix system.
    🔗
    3. Classifying the Equilibrium.
    Consider the system \(\vec{x}' = P\vec{x}\) where \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix}.\) How is the equilibrium at the origin classified?
    🔗
    • An unstable node.
    • Correct. Both eigenvalues \(3\) and \(1\) are positive real numbers, so solutions grow away from the origin along eigenvector directions, forming an unstable node.
    • A saddle point.
    • No. A saddle requires one positive and one negative eigenvalue.
    • A center.
    • No. Centers require purely imaginary eigenvalues.
    🔗
    4. Choose the Correct Phase Plane.
    Consider the system \(\vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}.\) Which of the following phase portraits corresponds to this system?
    🔗
    • (A) Unstable node
    • Correct. Both eigenvalues are positive (\(3\) and \(1\)) so the origin is an unstable node; trajectories move outward along the eigenvector directions (the coordinate axes).
    • (B) Saddle
    • Not this one. A saddle requires eigenvalues of opposite signs, which is not the case here.
    • (C) Center
    • Not this one. Centers correspond to purely imaginary eigenvalues (no real part), whereas both eigenvalues here are positive and real.
    🔗
    5. Describing the Vector Field and Trajectory Behavior.
    Consider the two-dimensional linear system \(\vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp -5 \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}.\) (a) Describe the vector field in the phase plane, including how the direction vectors \(P\vec{x}\) behave at various points. (b) Classify the equilibrium at the origin and explain the qualitative behavior of trajectories as \(t \to \infty\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The vector field assigns to each point \((x_1,x_2)\) the direction vector \(P\vec{x} = (-2x_1,\,-5x_2)\text{.}\) Because each component is a negative multiple of the coordinate, arrows everywhere in the plane point toward the origin. Along the \(x_1\)-axis, the flow is horizontal toward the origin at a rate proportional to \(-2x_1\text{.}\) Along the \(x_2\)-axis, the flow is vertical toward the origin but at a faster rate proportional to \(-5x_2\text{.}\) Off the axes, arrows point diagonally inward, with the \(x_2\)-component shrinking more quickly due to the larger magnitude eigenvalue. This interpretation follows the definition of the vector field as assigning \(P\vec{x}\) to each point.
    🔗
    (b) The eigenvalues of \(P\) are \(-2\) and \(-5\text{,}\) both negative. According to the classification in Section 7.5, a system with two negative real eigenvalues has a stable node at the origin. Hence all trajectories approach the origin as \(t \to \infty\text{.}\) Because \(-5\) has larger magnitude, the \(x_2\)-component decays faster, so trajectories tilt toward the \(x_1\)-axis as they approach the origin. This is consistent with the node behavior described in the section.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    6. Classifying a System Using Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors.
    Consider the system \(\vec{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp -4 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \vec{x}.\) (a) Compute the eigenvalues and classify the equilibrium at the origin. (b) Based on the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, describe qualitatively what the phase portrait looks like (e.g., rotation, spiraling, or approach/escape behavior).
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The characteristic equation is \(\det\!\left( \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp -4 \\ 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} - \lambda I \right) = \det \begin{bmatrix} -\lambda \amp -4 \\ 1 \amp -\lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 4 = 0.\) Therefore the eigenvalues are \(\lambda = \pm 2i\text{,}\) a pair of purely imaginary conjugates. Purely imaginary eigenvalues correspond to a center at the origin.
    🔗
    (b) With purely imaginary eigenvalues, solutions neither grow nor decay in magnitude. Instead, they rotate in closed or nearly closed orbits around the origin. The vector field shows arrows tangent to ellipses or circles, depending on the eigenvector geometry. In this case, the system behaves like a constant‑speed rotation, so the phase portrait consists of closed curves (center behavior). Such systems have trajectories that circle the equilibrium rather than approaching or diverging from it.
